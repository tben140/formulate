# Claims wording guide

How Double Helix may describe its products in **marketing**: push
notifications, email, the routine quiz, campaign banners, social posts.
Product data and labels follow the stricter rules in the
[catalogue notes](catalogue/double-helix/README.md). This guide applies the
same rules to everything around the products.

> ⚠️ Not legal advice. The wording below is **candidate** wording to check
> against the Great Britain nutrition and health claims register before use,
> together with each claim's conditions of use. Nothing goes live on a real
> trading store without review by someone qualified.

## The three rules

1. **Only authorised health claims, word for word or very close.** A health
   claim is anything saying or implying that a food or nutrient benefits
   health. In Great Britain, the only lawful ones are on the
   [GB nutrition and health claims register](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/great-britain-nutrition-and-health-claims-nhc-register).
   "Supports your immunity" is a rewording too far; "contributes to the
   normal function of the immune system" is the authorised form.
2. **No medicinal claims, ever.** Food supplements may not claim to prevent,
   treat or cure any disease or condition, or imply that they do (FSA
   guidance; CAP Code section 15). "Fixes low vitamin D", "beats anxiety" and
   "for joint pain" are all medicinal.
3. **The product must meet the claim's conditions.** Most vitamin and mineral
   claims need the daily portion to provide a significant amount of the
   nutrient, usually at least 15% of the reference intake (NRV). Some claims
   carry their own dose (creatine, omega-3). If the formula doesn't meet the
   condition, the claim can't be used, even if the nutrient is in the product.

A collection name is not a claim. "Mind & Focus" and "Cellular" are
navigation, and marketing copy mustn't turn them into a promise
("focus-boosting", "cell-renewing").

## By channel

**Push notifications and email subjects.** Short copy is where claims get
squeezed into slogans. If the claim doesn't fit in full, leave it out and say
something factual: "Vitamin D3 is back in stock", "Your magnesium runs out
around Friday".

**The routine quiz.** Questions are about **goals**, never symptoms ("What
would you like your routine to cover? Energy, focus, training…"), as ADR 0009
requires. Results name products and, where they exist, their authorised
claims. A goal isn't a promise: "for your focus goal: Magnesium Glycinate.
Magnesium contributes to normal psychological function" is fine; "Magnesium
will improve your focus" is not. A product with no authorised claim (see the
table) belongs in no goal.

**Campaigns and offers.**

- **Urgency must be true.** Countdowns end when the offer really ends, and
  "only 3 left" comes from real stock (Digital Markets, Competition and
  Consumers Act 2024).
- **Personal offers say they're personal** ("an offer for you"), rather than
  looking like a price change.
- **Mental-health moments** (Stress Awareness Month in April, Mental Health
  Awareness Week in May): support content and signposting only. No discounts,
  no product pushes tied to mood.
- **Seasonal claims keep their exact wording.** "Immunity season" is a theme,
  not a claim. The copy under it still uses the authorised vitamin D or C
  wording, or none.

**Reviews and testimonials.** Customer reviews (Judge.me) that make
medicinal claims mustn't be featured in marketing, even though the customer
wrote them. The same rules apply to anything the brand chooses to show.

## Review checklist

Before any copy ships:

- [ ] Every benefit statement matches an authorised claim on the register.
- [ ] The product's daily portion meets that claim's conditions.
- [ ] No disease, condition or symptom is mentioned or implied.
- [ ] No "probiotic", "detox", "boost", "cure", "anti-", "heal" or
      "prevent" wording.
- [ ] Any deadline or stock figure is real.
- [ ] Quiz and personalisation copy talks about goals, not symptoms.
- [ ] Copy around mental health has no offer attached.
